How to stop doomscrolling

Almost every guide on this opens by telling you to have more discipline. That advice fails for a specific, mechanical reason, and understanding the reason gets you further than another round of trying harder.

Why it happens, mechanically

A book has chapters. A television episode ends. A newspaper runs out of pages. Every older medium had a built-in moment where continuing required a decision, and that moment is where most people stopped.

An infinite feed removes it. There is no page you finish, no natural gap where you surface and think right, that's enough. Combine that with rewards arriving unpredictably — most posts are dull, occasionally one is great, and you cannot tell which is next — and you have the most reliably compelling pattern in behavioural psychology. Slot machines use it too.

You are not weak-willed. You are using a device engineered specifically to remove every place you could naturally stop.

Which reframes the task. You are not trying to want it less. You are trying to put the stopping point back.

What actually works

1. Add friction before the moment, not during it

Deciding to stop while mid-scroll is the hardest possible time to decide anything. Move the decision earlier, when you're calm: log out so signing back in takes effort, delete the app from your phone and use the browser version, turn your screen greyscale, or move the icon off the home screen. None of these stop you. Each adds a few seconds of deliberate action, which is often all it takes to notice what you're doing.

2. Give yourself an external stopping point

Since the feed won't end, something else has to. A timer works, but only if the alert is hard to dismiss on autopilot — a notification you can swipe away without reading is one you'll swipe away without reading. It needs to be something that actually interrupts the loop.

3. Replace the trigger, don't just block the behaviour

Doomscrolling is usually filling a gap: boredom between tasks, avoidance of something harder, or winding down. Block the scroll without addressing the gap and you'll find another one within a week. Decide in advance what fills that gap instead — a walk, a kettle, a specific tedious task — and the block has somewhere to send you.

4. Watch out for the blocker you'll uninstall

Hard blockers work brilliantly for a fortnight. Then a day comes when you genuinely need the blocked site, you hit the wall, you disable the extension — and you don't turn it back on. This is the single most common failure mode in this whole category, and it's worth designing around rather than pretending you're above it.

If you know you'll route around a wall, pick something gentler that survives contact with a bad day. A tool you still have running in six months beats a stricter one you disabled in week three.

5. Count the right thing

“Two hours on YouTube” means very little if the tab sat open in the background while you worked. Time that counts open tabs will tell you things that feel wrong, and once the numbers feel wrong you stop believing any of them. Look for something that measures the time you were actually looking at it.

6. Handle the autoplay problem

Video is the hardest case, because the next one starts on its own. A reminder that leaves the audio running isn't a break, it's a caption. Whatever you use needs to actually pause playback, or you'll read the reminder and keep watching.

7. Make the interruption pleasant

This sounds trivial and isn't. An interruption that feels like punishment creates resentment, and resentment is what gets tools uninstalled. An interruption you're glad to see gets to keep interrupting you. That is the entire design thesis behind putting a cat on the screen rather than an error page.

A reasonable place to start

Pick a limit you'd defend out loud — if 45 minutes on a site sounds fine, use 45, not 15 you'll resent by Tuesday. Apply it to the two or three sites that genuinely eat your day rather than everything. Decide now what you'll do when it goes off. Then leave it alone for two weeks before adjusting anything.

Where Cat Break fits

Cat Break is our attempt at points 2, 5, 6 and 7 in one free Chrome extension. It counts only the time a tab is actually focused, so background tabs never eat your allowance. When you cross your limit a cat walks onto the page, pauses whatever's playing, and stays for a break that ends itself. You can press Esc and carry on, deliberately — it's a speed bump, not a lock.

It isn't the right tool for everyone. If you need to be genuinely stopped, a hard blocker will serve you better, and we'd rather point you at StayFocusd or Cold Turkey than sell you something too gentle for the job. If the problem lives on your phone, no browser extension will help — Freedom covers phones and we don't.

Questions

Is doomscrolling actually harmful?

The research is still developing, but the consistent findings are on displacement and mood: time spent scrolling displaces sleep, exercise and in-person contact, and sustained exposure to distressing news is linked to worse mood and anxiety. The clearest harm for most people is simply the hours that go missing.

How long does it take to break the habit?

There is no reliable fixed number, and claims of 21 or 66 days are much shakier than they sound. A more useful frame is that the first week is the hardest and it gets easier once a replacement behaviour is established.

Does deleting the app work?

It works well as friction, less well as a solution, because most people reinstall or switch to the browser version. It is most effective combined with deciding what fills the gap the app was filling.

Are website blockers effective?

They work while you keep them on. The common failure is disabling one on a day you genuinely need the site and never re-enabling it, so choosing a strictness you can actually live with matters more than choosing the strictest option.
